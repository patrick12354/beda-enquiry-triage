The gate

A system that reads everything arriving in BEDA's inbox, works out which of two entirely different businesses it belongs to, and then refuses to send a single reply without a person.

Chapter I

Four doors, one room

Enquiries reach BEDA through a Wix form on the website, a shared mailbox, WhatsApp, and Instagram. Four doors into one room, and nothing that comes through them agrees on shape.

The form is the only structured one, and it collects six fields: name, location, current role, sales or marketing, LinkedIn, Instagram. Everything else arrives as prose written by a stranger who had no reason to be helpful.

So the first job is not intelligence. It is making four kinds of message into one kind of record, which is ordinary code, and it is where most of the reliability comes from.

Chapter II

The expensive mistake is not the one you would guess

BEDA is two businesses sharing an inbox. It recruits sales and marketing people in Bali, and it places them with Australian companies. So every message is from someone who wants to hire, or someone who wants to be hired.

Most triage systems are built to sort good leads from bad ones. That is the wrong axis here. The costly error is putting a candidate into the sales pipeline, or a client into the talent inbox. One is a privacy incident. The other is revenue that silently never gets a call back.

Here is a message that gets it wrong on the first read.

Chapter III

Most of it never reaches a model

A public form on the open internet collects link farms. A tripped honeypot, a blocklisted domain, five URLs in four lines: none of that needs a language model to recognise, and paying one to read it is both waste and an attack surface for no benefit.

So the cheapest stage runs first and it is plain code. What survives goes to a small model. Only the fraction that looks like real client demand, or that the small model was unsure about, is escalated to a bigger one.

The whole bill for a thousand enquiries a month is under a dollar and a half. Which is the real finding: at this volume, model cost is not the constraint. Human review minutes are.

Chapter IV

Four ways out, and no fifth

Whatever a model believes about a message, the system is only permitted to reach one of these four. Every one of them ends with a person, and the differences are how fast and how loud.

Outcome 01

File and notify

When
Complete, confident, and small enough
Does
Writes the record, pings the owner, drafts a reply
Sends
Nothing

Outcome 02

Ask

When
A required field is genuinely absent
Does
Drafts a question naming only the missing fields
Sends
Nothing

Outcome 03

Escalate

When
Low confidence, an ambiguous match, or a large enquiry
Does
Declines to decide and hands it to a named person
Sends
Nothing

Outcome 04

Quarantine

When
Junk, above a 0.95 confidence floor
Does
Writes no record, keeps the decision reversible
Sends
Nothing

The floor for calling something junk is the highest number in the system. Being wrong about spam means ignoring a real person, so it takes near certainty. Being unsure about anything else is cheap: it goes to a human.

    Chapter VI

    One thing I will not automate

    Rejecting a candidate. Not the sending of it. The decision, the ranking, and the score that would make either one easy to wave through.

    The system will read a candidate application, file it, extract the fields, alert the talent team and draft an acknowledgement. It will not decide that somebody is not good enough.

    A candidate here is not a lead. They are considering moving country. The form holds their name, their location, their LinkedIn and their Instagram handle, and those are proxies for nationality, gender, age and class. A model scoring on that input learns to discriminate and attaches a fluent justification to it, which is worse than a human bias because it is faster, consistent, and looks objective.

    It is also squarely inside automated-decision territory under both Indonesia's PDP law and the Australian Privacy Act, and BEDA operates across both.

    And it contradicts the product. BEDA's proposition is the power of good advice: coaching, mentorship, people who are built rather than born. A company whose whole pitch is that people deserve development cannot have its first act be an algorithm deciding somebody is not worth a reply.

    So that this reads as judgement rather than reflex: I am comfortable letting the system file records, route messages, discard obvious junk and enrich company data on its own. Those are reversible, they touch nobody's dignity, and requiring approval for them would only train the team to click approve without reading, which is how approval queues die.
